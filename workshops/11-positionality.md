# Positionality

![Reflexivity means looking back at you (the researcher), your decisions and methods, and acknowledging how they are linked.](img/reflection.jpg){alt="A reflection of the clouds in windows." width=80%}

This tutorial is about you, the researcher, and how your decisions and actions connect to what happens in your final project. 
Every HCI project is a sequence of research decisions: what to prototype, who to recruit, what methods to use, and what results might be called a finding. These decisions don't happen automatically, they are made by someone with a particular training, history, body, and set of relationships, and that someone is the instrument through which the project's knowledge is produced. _Positionality_ is the practice of owning those decisions and communicating how your position shaped them. Olmos-Vega and colleagues wrote that reflexivity "should go beyond disclosing each investigator's background and training; it should include descriptions of how the researcher's prior experiences and motivations might influence the decisions made throughout the project" [@olmos-vega-reflexivity-qualitative-research:2023].

For COMP6390 students this tutorial prepares you to develop the positionality statement in the [final project](https://smcclab.au/thirty-nine-hundred-hci/assessments/03-prototype-and-evaluation.html), which is marked on how  you connect your background and presence to decisions in your research. For COMP3900 students these skills will help you to evaluate your findings critically and would prepare you for any further study or projects in HCI.

Your final project will be at a different stage to your classmates' which is totally fine. This class works on the decisions you have made so far, and the ones you have not made yet are a good place to look for alternatives.

## Pre-Class Tasks

You must complete these tasks before the start of your tutorial.

1. Re-read the [reflexivity section of the week 6 lecture](https://smcclab.au/thirty-nine-hundred-hci/lectures/06-reflexive-thematic-analysis.html#/reflexivity), then read the _four types of reflexivity_ (personal, interpersonal, methodological, contextual) in @olmos-vega-reflexivity-qualitative-research:2023 and the positionality statement in @liang-tensions-hci-marginalized:2021. You will need this knowledge to evaluate other researcher's statements and craft your own.

2. Find **one published HCI paper that contains an explicit positionality or reflexivity statement**. These have become common at SIGCHI venues since around 2021: a scoping review of CHI found 272 papers with full positionality statements over eleven years, only 14 of them before 2021 and 111 in 2024 alone [@singh-positionality-hci:2025]. So a full-text search in the [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/) for "positionality" or "our positionality", filtered to SIGCHI sponsored conferences or journals, will find heaps. Look through the whole paper, not just the statement, because you need to judge whether the statement matches what the authors actually did.

    The paper **must not** be one that appears in the lecture slides, workshops, or reading list for this course. Part of the task is going into the literature on your own.

3. Post on the forum (100--200 words, with at least one proper reference to your paper):

    - Cite the paper and quote or paraphrase the positionality statement in one or two sentences.
    - Which of Olmos-Vega's four types of reflexivity does the statement address?
    - What epistemological stance does the paper take (see the [lecture's list](https://smcclab.au/thirty-nine-hundred-hci/lectures/06-reflexive-thematic-analysis.html#/epistemology) for examples)? Is it declared or did you have to infer it? Are the methods and claims coherent with it?
    - Name **one specific decision in the paper** (a design choice, a recruitment choice, an analysis choice, an interpretation) that the authors' position plausibly shaped. Was it acknowledged in the paper?

4. Bring your current final project plan to class: your research question, prototype idea, method, participant plan, and any work on analysis you have done so far. If parts are undecided, bring the options you are considering.

The last bullet of task 3 is tricky, but it's also the specific skill being assessed in the positionality rubric for COMP6390 students. Linking a position to a decision, rather than just describing your position, makes positionality a useful part of a research work, not just a biography.

## In-Class Tasks

### Task 0: Biography or positionality? (10 mins)

In groups of 4--5, each person gives a one-sentence summary of the statement they found. As a group, place each statement on a line from **biography** at one end (e.g., "we are three computer scientists at a US university") to **decision-linked** at the other (e.g., "because two of us are former nurses, we designed the interview protocol around the existing tensions of shift handover").

Identify how the decision-linked statements contribute to evaluating the authors' research. The same scoping review found that statements are "often a box-ticking exercise and their influence on the research is rarely discussed" [@singh-positionality-hci:2025], so expect most of what you found to sit near the biography end. Share these with the class, and compare them with the final project rubric row, which rewards reflection that "clearly articulates how background and presence shaped specific decisions at each stage".

> **A note on disclosure.** Positionality is "not necessarily about disclosing your identity" (week 6 lecture). Some researchers choose to own aspects of their identity, e.g., cultural background, (dis)abilities, lived experience of the research context, because it changed what they did and why. That can be powerful, but it is not required for an effective statement. The researchers interviewed by Liang, Munson and Kientz [-@liang-tensions-hci-marginalized:2021] describe the discomfort of positionality being read as a demand to "out ourselves at the beginning of every paper", and that is not what is being asked here. Positionality is about understanding how your position, your decisions, and your actions are linked, and showing that the links were not automatic.

### Task 1: Decision audit (20 mins)

Work individually in the _shared document provided by your tutor_. There is one table per student, with one row for each stage of the final project. Fill in as many rows as you can. Rows for stages you haven't reached yet are still useful: write what you are leaning towards and why.

| Stage | What I chose (or am choosing) | What I did not choose | Why the choice felt natural to me | Who would have chosen differently, and what would they have done? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Design concept and user needs | | | | |
| Prototype form and fidelity | | | | |
| Research question | | | | |
| Evaluation method and data type | | | | |
| Participants and recruitment | | | | |
| Analysis approach | | | | |
| What counts as a finding | | | | |

"Felt natural" can be a signal: choices that seem obvious to you might be explained by your position. Some prompts:

- Your training: what does a computing degree make easy to measure, and what does it make invisible? What would a lawyer, a musician, a gardener, or an eight-year-old have measured?
- Your relationship to the context: are you an insider to the activity you are studying, an outsider, or a former insider? What does each position let you see and miss?
- Your participants: they are your classmates. What do you know about them that an external researcher wouldn't, and what do they know about you?
- Your body and circumstances: your language, your access needs, where you live, when you are free. Which of these set the time, place, or format of your study?

<!-- Your tutor will drop in to discuss the audits. Having a weak or empty "did not choose" column is the most common problem: if you can't name an alternative, you haven't yet found the decision. -->

### Task 2: Epistemology check (15 mins)

In pairs. Each of you declares an epistemological stance for your project from the [list in the week 6 lecture](https://smcclab.au/thirty-nine-hundred-hci/lectures/06-reflexive-thematic-analysis.html#/epistemology) (post-positivist, interpretivist, constructionist, critical, pragmatist, new materialist). Then test it for **coherence** [@braun-thematic-analysis:2022]:

1. What kind of claim do you plan to make from your findings? ("Users completed the task faster", "participants experienced the prototype as intrusive", "the prototype reconfigured who held control of the activity")
2. How will you show the claim is trustworthy? What counts as quality depends on your stance. A post-positivist study shows it with counts, statistics, codebooks and inter-rater agreement: the aim is that another researcher would get the same result. Reflexive TA has no equivalent tool. For Braun and Clarke [-@braun-thematic-analysis:2022] quality is thinking carefully and showing your work: thorough, repeated coding rather than themes built from a few vivid quotes; extracts that evidence every analytic claim; a convincing, well-organised story that answers the research question; a reflexive journal that records how your assumptions shaped the analysis; and a report in which what you claim to have done matches what you did.
3. Do 1 and 2 match the stance you declared? If you declared an interpretivist stance and plan to report inter-rater reliability, or a post-positivist stance and plan to report "themes that emerged", there's a mismatch. Braun and Clarke call the unnoticed slide of positivist assumptions (objectivity, "bias" as something to control, a single truth to find) into qualitative research "positivism creep". Decide how to fix it.

If you are collecting quantitative data, this task is not a formality. A post-positivist stance makes claims about an objective reality, so your positionality statement is about what your instrument measures, what it does not, who counts as a user in your sample, and why you chose that measure over another. That is still a decision shaped by your position.

Write your declared stance and one sentence on its coherence into your row of the shared document.

### Task 3: Interview the researcher (20 mins)

Still in pairs. Take turns, ten minutes each: one of you is the researcher, the other is the interviewer. The interviewer's job is to find decisions the researcher hasn't noticed making, using the audit from task 1 as the starting point. The researcher takes notes on their own answers, because the notes are the raw material for task 4.

Some example interviewer questions:

- Why that method and not this alternative? (_make sure you think of an alternative!_) When did you decide?
- Who is not in your participant group? Why not? What would they say about your prototype?
- Your participants are classmates who will also be studying you. What do you hold over them, and they over you? How does that change what they will say to you?
- Is there a group you belong to that your participants don't, or vice versa? Where did that show up in your study design?
- What finding are you hoping for? What would you do if the data says the opposite?
- Which of your decisions would your tutor, your participants, or a reviewer at CHI push back on first?
- What does your project take for granted about how people use technology that someone from a different background might not?

The interviewer should push past the first answer. "I chose a survey because it's efficient" is a biography answer. "I chose a survey because I'm uncomfortable running interviews in my second language. I understand that a survey will give me less about participants' feelings, which is what my research question is about" is a positionality answer, and specifies a tension between data and findings.

This is **interpersonal reflexivity** in action. Pay attention to how it feels to be asked these questions by a peer.

### Task 4: Draft and peer-mark a statement (20 mins)

Individually (10 mins): write a draft positionality statement of about 150 words for your final project, using your audit and interview notes. Use this sentence pattern as scaffolding and then make it your own:

> **Position** → **decision** → **consequence.** _Because I [position], I [decided X rather than Y], which means [consequence for what the project can and cannot find]._

Two examples, one owning an aspect of identity and one not. Both work because of the links, not the disclosure:

- _As someone who uses a screen reader, I designed the prototype's voice feedback first and the visual layer second, which means my evaluation is well placed to find audio timing problems but my sighted participants may be reacting to a visual layer I never fully tested._
- _I am a final-year computing student with no experience of the studio art context I am studying, so I chose a structured task-completion study rather than observing participants' own practice; this means I can report completion times with some confidence but have little access to how the tool would contribute to the users' real practice._

As an example of a statement to avoid: "I am a CS student so I prefer quantitative methods, and I like games so I used gamification." This type is a biography with the decisions missing. Where is the alternative not chosen, and what what was lost or gained by these choices?

Swap with your partner (10 mins): mark each other's draft against the [final project rubric row for the positionality statement](https://smcclab.au/thirty-nine-hundred-hci/assessments/03-prototype-and-evaluation.html#rubric). Which band is it in? Point to the specific sentence that would move it up a band. COMP3900 students' drafts are not part of the assessment, but the marking exercise is the same and the draft is a good basis for the limitations in your findings.

Paste your draft into the shared document.

### Task 5: Wrap up (5 mins)

**For COMP6390 students:** positionality statements are required in your video (and your document). If it's missing in the video you get zero for positionality!

A spoken paragraph over a slide listing your position and the connected decisions is enough.

For everybody: tell your tutor and the class (out loud) something you have discovered about your research today that you hadn't noticed before.

> **Extension: first-person methods.** Positionality treats the researcher's position as something to account for. A growing strand of HCI goes further and treats the researcher's own experience as the primary data: autoethnography, autobiographical design, and soma design, which the TOCHI special issue on first-person methods maps out [@desjardins-first-person-methods:2021] and which Höök and colleagues argue for in soma-based design [@hook-first-person-soma:2018]. If your final project is about something you do yourself (make music, draw, exercise, game), read the special issue introduction and ask whether a first-person component would strengthen your evaluation, and what you would need to declare to make it rigorous rather than merely personal.
