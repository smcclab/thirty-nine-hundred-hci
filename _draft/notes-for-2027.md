# Notes for 2027

offering of COMP3900/COMP6390. Add to this during semester while the pain is

## Assessment

### A2 "AI Needs Finding" (30%, due week 10, 2026)

*Added 20 Oct 2026, after marking A2.*

- **Hard to distinguish good from bad work.** Needs-finding reports cluster
  tightly in quality: most students run a small study, produce plausible
  themes, and write them up competently. The rubric does not separate strong
  from weak submissions well, so marks compress into a narrow band and the
  30% weighting is doing less work than it should.
- **Marking is dreadfully boring.** 180-odd reports on the same brief with the
  same structure are tedious to mark and hard to mark consistently. Tutor
  fatigue is a real risk to reliability.
- **The practice is still needed.** Students do need to run a user-research
  study (recruit, consent, collect, analyse, report) before the final project.
  The question is whether a 30% report is the right vehicle, not whether the
  skill matters.

Alternatives to consider for 2027:

- **Charles's preferred direction (20 Oct 2026):** raise the in-class
  assessments from 10% to 20% so the weekly research practice carries real
  stakes, and back that with better support in tutorials (scaffolded
  recruiting, consent, data collection and analysis steps across weeks 6–8).
  Then change A2's output to a more **authentic form** rather than a short
  written report. The short reports are "just incredibly nothing": they look
  like a report, read like a report, and tell you almost nothing about what
  the student can do. Candidate authentic forms: a research presentation or
  briefing to a design team, an annotated data pack with analysis trail, a
  design-requirements handover that A3 must then consume, a poster or
  critique session. **A2 drops from 30% to 20%** to fund the in-class
  increase, giving class tasks 20%, A1 20%, A2 20%, A3 40%.

- Make the research practice a smaller, lower-stakes task (or a sequence of
  in-tutorial tasks across weeks 6–8) and move weight to A3, where the
  research feeds a design.
- Vary the brief or the deliverable (e.g. a short presentation, a poster, a
  data-analysis artefact, a peer-reviewed study plan) so submissions are less
  uniform and more discriminating.
- Assess the *process* evidence (raw data, analysis trail, reflection on what
  went wrong) rather than the polished report, which is where differences
  between students actually show.
- Keep the 2026 data-integrity additions (Participants section, raw data in
  `materials/`, spot-check contacts) whatever form the task takes.

### In-class tasks (10%, 2026) — evidence from Canvas, 20 Oct 2026

Marks for weeks 2–9 (eight 1-point tasks, 184 active students, week 9 final
with missing grades zeroed):

- Weekly means 0.66–0.89; full marks 64–86% of the class each week; partial
  credit under 10% in every week — the marks are effectively **binary**
  (did it / didn't), so the component measures participation, not quality.
- Per-student totals (max 8): mean 6.31, median 7, SD 1.99, quartiles
  5.75 / 7 / 8. Sixty students on 8, fifty on 7 (60% of class in top two
  bins); long thin tail with 22 students at 3 or below.
- Week 9 (due 9 Oct, in the A2 crunch) dropped to 64% full / 33% zero — the
  1-point task loses whenever it collides with a major deadline.

Implications for a 20% in-class component: the current scheme would only
spread the disengaged tail further; to discriminate among the top 60% the
weekly research tasks need graded criteria (which is where the "better
support" and tutor marking load must be designed together). Higher stakes
should also reduce the deadline-collision triage seen in week 9.
