Each assignment in this course gives you a scenario, a task, and a specification. The assessment do not tell what you your design should be or exactly how to implement it. The gap between the a reasonably open assignment prompt and your specific design is where the work happens. 
Differently than some other computing courses, this work is more related to *choosing what to do* (and then doing it), than *implementing a a specification*.
Uncertainty about how to make those design choices are leading to a lot of questions. For assignment 1, about a third of questions were of the form "is X allowed?" or "is my idea OK?". This page is about how to answer that kind of question yourself.

## Two kinds of question {#two-kinds}

Some questions have a factual answer in the specification. Do the sketches need to be hand-drawn? How many designs? Is cardboard allowed? What reference format? Can I use an SUS questionnaire? If you cannot find the answer in the spec, ask on the forum and I will answer it (and usually fix the spec). These questions are very important and worth asking.

The other kind of question does not have a set answer. Is my task specific enough? Can I assume away a constraint from my user's context? Does my design need to be buildable today? Should my survey include a small experiment? These are _design decisions_. If I answer them for you, I have made part of your design, and the rationale you write afterwards will be explaining my choice rather than yours. The rubrics in this course reward a defended choice, not a permitted one. So when I get one of these questions my answer is usually a version of "that is a decision for you to make and defend", plus some thoughts on how to make it.

As a quick test: if the answer would be different for a different student with a different design, it is a design decision.

Before you stop asking questions: I am happy to discuss design decisions with you on the forum! I just want to be clear that [much like Elves](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/756102-but-it-is-said-do-not-meddle-in-the-affairs)) I will not give a clear answer on what you should do, but I will help you understand the the consequences of design decisions and how to make them in a structure way.

## How to make a design decision {#how}

1. **Find what the scenario fixed, and what it left open.** In each assignment there is a brief prompt with a scenario setting certain aspects of the world (e.g., animals are smart) with everything else in the world remaining the same as now. These are your constraints. Everything in your design (prototype, needs finding study, prototype and evaluation) is open and up to you to decide within those constraints.

2. **Rule out the moves that rewrite the brief.** If your user's habitat rules out the obvious hardware, "assume the habitat is not a problem" changes the scenario, and "assume the technology I need exists" changes the world. So these choices rewrite the brief and aren't valid. A speculative technology is fine if it is grounded in what is actually known, and you acknowledge the gap as a limitation. A "magical" solution that solves the task perfectly will produce a thin prototype and rationale, as the A1 spec warns.

3. **Pick options you can argue for, not the safest one.** Choose the one that produces the most interesting design and that you can support from the lectures, readings, and your own research about your users. A user whose body or context is very different from the designer's is harder to design for, and a harder user usually gives you more to say. A narrow task gives you more to say than a broad one, because you can get concrete about the actual interactions that might occur.

4. **Write the decision down, with the reasoning and the trade-off.** The decision goes in your rationale or study plan, along with why you made it and what it costs. You are allowed to conclude that a human user loses something in your redesign, or that your survey has too few participants for a statistical test. What you are not supposed to do is leave the trade-off implicit or assumed to be known to the marker. Be clear about your decisions because you are being marked on them!

5. **Check the result still answers the brief.** After making your choices, go back to the specification and confirm that your design still does what was asked: e.g., both users can do the task, the study still answers the client's research question, the prototype still shows the interaction. A creative choice that drifts away from the brief is not a good assignment solution.

## Some examples {#examples}

These are the kinds of question that can come up (these are drawn from the 2025 and 2026 forums), with the shape of the answer.

### Any assignment {#examples-general}

- _My user's habitat or body rules out the hardware I would normally reach for. Can I assume that away?_ Ignoring the constraint weakens the rationale, and changing the scenario is not available, so engaging with the constraint is part of what makes the design interesting. Say how in the rationale.
- _Is a task that most people never do still an everyday task?_ Everyday means routine and recurring for the people who do it, not administrative or universal. The decision is then how much context of use to establish for a marker who may never have seen the task.
- _Does the design need to be physically possible?_ It does not need to be a product today, but the physics is unchanged. A speculative technology is a stronger idea when grounded in what is actually known about the user and the technology, with gaps acknowledged as a limitation. A "magical" technology that makes the design problem disappear leaves you nothing to design.
- _Is my idea too simple? A tutor said they would prefer something more complex._ Complexity is not a marking criterion; quality is. A simple, compelling, well-executed idea beats a complicated one you cannot prototype or test properly. If the idea meets the specification, the decision about how far to push it is yours.
- _Can I use Excel / a different tool / a method not taught in the course?_ Nothing is banned, but the course teaches defaults (Python with pandas and seaborn for analysis, paper prototyping for prototypes) and everyone has practised these defaults in tutorials. Using alternatives is your call: it is fine if it genuinely improves the work and you can support the choice, and a risk if it produces something you cannot analyse or test.
- _Can I put the details in the materials folder, or link to an external site, and keep the main document short?_ Factual: the main document (and in A3, the video) is what gets marked. Materials exist so the marker can verify your work, not discover it. External links are not followed. If a figure, quote, or result matters, it goes in the main document.
- _Can I reuse references from an earlier assignment?_ Factual: yes, as in any research project. Reframe the justification for the new context.
- _How should I acknowledge generative AI use?_ Factual: a short list in the Acknowledgements section of what you used it for is enough. Full prompts are not needed. Undeclared use, and references that do not exist, fall under the N category. The design decision is whether to use it at all: the marker is looking for evidence of _your_ process, and generic AI-generated sketches or text don't show that.

### Assignment 2: needs finding {#examples-a2}

- _Can I narrow my study to one specific task or feature of the AI tool, rather than the whole tool?_ Yes, and you should. The specification asks for one context. One well-observed task gives you depth; many tasks give you breadth and thin data from 3--5 people. Explain why the context you chose is worth studying.
- _Is X too small or too niche to count as an AI use context?_ If it fits the specification (students using AI in a specific situation in their lives) it is allowed. Whether it is a _good_ choice is your decision and part of what is marked in the study plan: a well-justified narrow context is stronger than a vague broad one.
- _Should my needs-finding study be designed around a statistical test?_ No. Design it to answer the research question. With three to five participants, descriptive statistics and clear plots are the appropriate analysis. Run a test only if a meaningful comparison arises, and be honest about its power.
- _Can I use a validated instrument (SUS, NASA-TLX), adapt one, or add a small task or experiment?_ You can borrow from existing instruments and include a task, if the framing stays needs finding and you still gather participants' own accounts of what they need. Adapting an instrument (changing its scale, dropping items) is acceptable if you say what you changed and why. Remember that the research question is given; you are answering it, not pursuing your own interests.
- _Can my qualitative data come from open-ended survey questions instead of interviews?_ It can. Consider what you lose: needs finding depends on participants' own accounts, and a survey box rarely gives you the follow-up that reveals a need. If you go this way, plan for it in your analysis.
- _Should I ask the same questions before and after a task?_ A reasonable design, and one of many. The choice depends on your context and what you want to learn. Explain the procedure and why you chose it.
- _My data does not split cleanly into quantitative and qualitative. Which section does it go in?_ Organise by the _analysis_ you did, not by how the data looked when you collected it. Quantitative analysis in the quantitative section, qualitative analysis in the qualitative section. This maps to the rubric.
- _How many themes should I have? How many quotes?_ There is no number but follow Charles' rule of four (probably no more than four themes, each theme should probably have four or more words). Three well-supported themes that say something important beats eight that are really categories. A theme is a finding: it needs quotes, an argument, and a statement of why it matters for the research question. Codes and affinity diagrams are evidence of process; they are not the result.
- _Is a photo of the interview enough evidence, or do I need transcripts?_ Factual: raw data goes in the materials folder. Transcripts or recordings, survey exports, photos of handwritten notes. A summary is not raw data. The marker needs to be convinced the findings came from the data.
- _My numbers are all in the report. Why is the quantitative section marked as thin?_ Numbers are not findings. A mean or a plot indicates a finding that you then have to explain: what it says about the participants, the context, and the research question. Restating a table in prose is not analysis.
- _Can I recruit participants from outside the class?_ Factual: no. All participants must be current COMP3900/6390 students, listed by uid. If recruiting goes wrong, talk to staff early; a small honest study beats a fabricated one.

### Assignment 3: prototype and evaluation {#examples-a3}

- _Does X count as non-standard interaction? (computer vision, AR, barcode scanning, voice, haptics, a physical device...)_ Mostly factual: the test is whether the interaction goes beyond a web app operated only by keyboard, mouse, or touchscreen. All of those examples pass. The _amount_ of non-standard interaction is not a marking criterion; it is a design prompt to keep you away from yet another Figma web app. What is marked is the quality of the prototype.
- _Can I have more than one focus (energy and waste, say)?_ You can, but be careful how much you attempt with one prototype and one small evaluation. Making that scoping choice well is part of the assignment.
- _I have three research questions. Is that OK?_ The specification says _a_ research question: one, incorporating your area of interaction, a problem, and a justification. Several questions usually means the study is not yet focused. If you designed the prototype first and are now looking for a question, frame the RQ as the inquiry that led to the design.
- _Can I prototype the non-standard interaction as a simulation on a touchscreen or in a game engine? Software or hardware? Paper or code?_ Any of these can work. The requirement is that the prototype lets you _test_ the interaction you designed with a participant. Figma alone rarely does this for non-standard interaction. Paper prototyping is the safe baseline everyone has practised; code or hardware is fine if you have the skills and it stays testable.
- _How much of a Wizard of Oz prototype has to be real?_ Some of it. The expectation is that not everything works, but the parts that carry the experience you are evaluating should be real enough for a participant to react to. A real spray of scent, a real LED you switch on, a real sound from a phone will elicit better data than a box labelled "aroma dispenser". Deciding which parts to make real is a prototyping skill and part of the assessment.
- _My prototype has light, sound, or touch feedback but is paper-based. How do I test those?_ Coloured paper, sticky notes, an LED or torch, a sound played from a phone, and a wizard behind the curtain. There is no set answer: designing a prototype that enables testing is the task.
- _Do I need to show sketches, iterations, and design process like in A1?_ Not to the same extent. The prototype criterion rewards a thorough design and prototyping process _with consideration of user needs_ and a clear demonstration of the interaction in video. Show enough process that your design decisions are legible, then spend the time on the prototype and how it is tested. A real photo or video of the built prototype beats a drawing of it.
- _My evaluation shows the prototype does not work. Do I need to build a version 2?_ No. Iteration is out of scope. Null or negative findings are fine if the data collection and analysis are valid. Reflect on whether the prototype and plan were testable in the first place; if they were, report honestly what you found.
- _With five participants, should I run independent groups, repeated measures, or just one condition?_ Repeated measures is normal at this scale and gives you plenty of room to show rigour (counterbalanced ordering, for example). Independent groups with two or three per condition is possible but thin. Comparing against an existing interface is also an acceptable approach. Pick one and justify it.
- _What counts as "justification from HCI academic research"?_ Both kinds: sources that describe the method you apply, and sources from similar projects that used it, ideally pointing to a gap your study addresses.
- _Where do the conclusions go?_ Factual: in the first section, "Research Question, Plan, and Conclusions". That is standard academic practice (introductions state the findings) and it saves you writing a separate conclusion. The headings are not flexible because they map directly to the marking criteria.
- _Can I change the headings for better narrative flow?_ Factual: no. You can shorten them to fit a slide and add sub-headings underneath, but keep the structure.
- _My spoken transcript is only 600 words. Is that too short for an 1800-word document?_ Factual: no. The document is secondary to the video and may be a transcript or slightly different text. The extra words are for captions, tables, and small elaborations, not a second, longer report.
- _COMP6390: does the positionality statement go in the video?_ Factual: yes, and in the document. If it is absent from the video it scores zero.
- _Can I show the prototype working during the video, edit multiple takes, or include a demo clip?_ Factual: yes to all. Everything counts toward the 5-minute limit and must be inside the one submitted video; external videos are not watched.

## When to ask anyway {#when-to-ask}

If you're still unsure, I'm always open to questions and discussion! It's especially important to ask questions when you cannot find a fact in the spec or the spec seems to be contradictory, or when you have made a decision and want to check that it still answers the brief. 
For that last kind of question, post your decision and your reasoning rather than the bare question. "I am doing X because Y; does this still meet the spec?" is much easier to answer than "can I do X?". If your question includes details of your design, you can post privately, but if it is general I will usually ask if it can be made public so that others benefit.
