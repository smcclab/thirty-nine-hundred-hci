title: Making Choices in a Design Project
author: Dr Charles Martin

Each assignment in this course gives you a scenario, a task, and a specification. It does not give you a design. The gap between the two is where the work happens, and it is where a lot of questions come from. Reading through the forum during Assignment 1, about a third of the questions were of the form "is X allowed?" or "is my idea OK?". This page is about how to answer that kind of question yourself.

## Two kinds of question {#two-kinds}

Some questions have a factual answer in the specification. Do the sketches need to be hand-drawn? How many designs? Is cardboard allowed? What reference format? If you cannot find the answer in the spec, ask on the forum and I will answer it (and usually fix the spec). These questions are worth asking.

The other kind of question does not have a set answer. Is my task specific enough? Can I assume away a constraint from my user's context? Does my design need to be buildable today? Should my survey include a small experiment? These are _design decisions_. If I answer them for you, I have made part of your design, and the rationale you write afterwards will be explaining my choice rather than yours. The rubric rewards a defended choice, not a permitted one. So when I get one of these questions my answer is usually a version of "that is a decision for you to make and defend", plus some thoughts on how to make it.

The quick test: if the answer would be different for a different student with a different design, it is a design decision.

## How to make a design decision {#how}

1. **Find what the scenario fixed, and what it left open.** In Assignment 1, a burst of neutrinos gave animals human-level intelligence and a wish to join human society. That is the only change. Physics is the same, the animal's body is the same, and the technology that exists today is what exists. Everything the scenario did not change is a real constraint, and everything it did not specify is yours to decide. The same applies in Assignment 2, where you are a contracted researcher with a client's research question, and in the final project, where you have a sustainability brief and a requirement for non-standard interaction.

2. **Rule out the moves that rewrite the brief.** If your user's habitat rules out the obvious hardware, "assume the habitat is not a problem" changes the scenario, and "assume the technology I need exists" changes the world. Neither is available. A speculative technology is fine if it is grounded in what is actually known, and you acknowledge the gap as a limitation. A "magical" solution that solves the task perfectly will produce a thin prototype and a thinner rationale, as the spec warns.

3. **Pick the option you can argue for, not the safest one.** Of the options left, choose the one that produces the most interesting design and that you can support from the lectures, readings, and your own research about your users. A user whose body or context is very different from the designer's is harder to design for, and a harder user usually gives you more to say. A narrow task gives you more to say than a broad one, because you can get concrete about the actual moment of interaction.

4. **Write the decision down, with the reasoning and the trade-off.** The decision goes in your rationale or study plan, along with why you made it and what it costs. You are allowed to conclude that a human user loses something in your redesign, or that your survey has too few participants for a statistical test. What you are not allowed to do is leave the trade-off invisible. An explicit trade-off is design reasoning; a hidden one looks like an oversight.

5. **Check the result still answers the brief.** After making the choice, go back to the specification and confirm that your design still does what was asked: both users can do the task, the study still answers the client's research question, the prototype still shows the interaction. A creative choice that drifts away from the brief is the most common way a good idea loses marks.

## Some examples {#examples}

These are the kinds of question that come up every year, with the shape of the answer.

- _My user's habitat or body rules out the hardware I would normally reach for. Can I assume that away?_ Design decision. Ignoring the constraint weakens the rationale, and changing the scenario is not available, so engaging with the constraint is part of what makes the design interesting. Say how in the rationale.
- _Is a task that most people never do still an everyday task?_ Everyday means routine and recurring for the people who do it, not administrative or universal. The decision is then how much context of use to establish for a marker who may never have seen the task.
- _Does the design need to be physically possible?_ It does not need to be a product today, but the physics is unchanged. A speculative technology is a stronger idea when grounded in what is actually known about the user and the technology, with the gap acknowledged as a limitation.
- _Should my needs-finding study be designed around a statistical test?_ No. Design it to answer the research question. With three to five participants, descriptive statistics and clear plots are the appropriate analysis. Run a test only if a meaningful comparison arises, and be honest about its power.
- _Can I use a validated instrument, or add a small experiment, in my needs-finding study?_ You can borrow from existing instruments and include a task, if the framing stays needs finding and you still gather participants' own accounts of what they need. Remember that in Assignment 2 you are answering a client's question, not following your own interests.

## When to ask anyway {#when-to-ask}

Ask when you cannot find a fact in the spec, when two parts of the spec seem to contradict each other, or when you have made a decision and want to check that it still answers the brief. For that last kind of question, post your decision and your reasoning rather than the bare question. "I am doing X because Y; does this still meet the spec?" is much easier to answer than "can I do X?", and writing it out is most of the work of the rationale anyway. If your question includes details of your design, you can post privately, but if it is general I will usually ask if it can be made public so that others benefit.
